# The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES)

- Document: c09 (course)
- Source: Written for this project from the topics of the CoSc4035 Computer Security course slides (Jimma University), Chapter 3, part 1
- Licence: CC BY 4.0

By the late 1990s a replacement for DES was needed: its key was too short, and triple DES was slow and still used small 64-bit blocks. In 1997 the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) called for proposals. The new cipher had to be a symmetric block cipher with 128-bit blocks and keys of 128, 192 or 256 bits, stronger and faster than triple DES, with full design details and implementations in C and Java.

Fifteen candidates were accepted in June 1998, and five were shortlisted in August 1999: MARS, RC6, Rijndael, Serpent and Twofish. Rijndael, designed by the Belgian cryptographers Vincent Rijmen and Joan Daemen, was selected in October 2000 and published as the standard FIPS PUB 197 in November 2001. It is now known as the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES).

Unlike DES, AES is not a Feistel cipher. It works on the whole 128-bit block in every round, treating it as a 4 x 4 table of bytes called the state. The number of rounds depends on the key length: 10 rounds for a 128-bit key, 12 for a 192-bit key and 14 for a 256-bit key. The key is first expanded into a set of round keys. Processing starts with an AddRoundKey step, and each full round then has four steps:
- SubBytes: each byte is replaced using a 16 x 16 lookup table called the S-box. This step provides confusion.
- ShiftRows: the rows of the state are shifted to the left in a circular way. The first row stays the same, and the second, third and fourth rows move by one, two and three bytes.
- MixColumns: each column is mixed so that every byte depends on all four bytes of its column. Together with ShiftRows, this provides diffusion.
- AddRoundKey: the state is XORed with the round key.
The final round leaves out MixColumns.

AES is more secure than DES against brute-force attacks because of its longer keys, and it was designed to resist the statistical and linear attacks known from DES. Its operations are simple enough to run efficiently on cheap processors with little memory.
