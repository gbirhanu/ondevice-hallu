# Security goals and basic terms

- Document: c01 (course)
- Source: Written for this project from the topics of the CoSc4035 Computer Security course slides (Jimma University), Chapter 1
- Licence: CC BY 4.0

Computer security means protecting an organisation's information system resources (its hardware, software, firmware, data and communication links) so that they stay useful to the people who are allowed to use them. Three goals are at the centre of the subject, often called the CIA triad.

Confidentiality means that information is disclosed only to people who are authorised to see it. This includes protecting personal privacy and business secrets. When confidentiality is lost, information has been disclosed without permission.

Integrity means that information is not changed or destroyed in an improper way. It also covers authenticity and non-repudiation. When integrity is lost, information has been modified or destroyed without permission.

Availability means that authorised users can reach and use information and systems reliably and on time. When availability is lost, access to the information or the system is disrupted.

A few other terms are used throughout the course:
- A vulnerability is a weakness in the design, implementation, operation or management of a system that could be used to break its security policy.
- A threat is any person, event or thing that could cause harm by exploiting a vulnerability. The harm does not have to happen for the threat to exist.
- An attack is a deliberate action by an intelligent adversary who tries to evade security services and break a system's security policy.
- A countermeasure is anything (an action, a device, a procedure or a technique) that lowers a threat, a vulnerability or an attack. It may stop it, reduce the harm it does, or detect and report it so that someone can respond.
- Risk is the loss you can expect. It is expressed as the chance that a given threat will exploit a given vulnerability and lead to a given harmful result.
- A security policy is the set of rules and practices that state how a system or organisation provides security services to protect its sensitive resources.

Vulnerabilities come in several kinds: physical (for example buildings), natural (earthquakes), hardware and software (failures), media (disks that can be stolen), communication (wires that can be tapped) and human (insiders).
