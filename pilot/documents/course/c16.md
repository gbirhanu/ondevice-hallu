# The Orange Book (TCSEC) and later criteria

- Document: c16 (course)
- Source: Written for this project from the topics of the CoSc4035 Computer Security course slides (Jimma University), Chapters 6 and 7
- Licence: CC BY 4.0

The Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria (TCSEC), developed for the US Department of Defense and known as the Orange Book after the colour of its cover, was the first set of standards used to grade how secure commercial computer products are. It was later extended to networks in the Trusted Network Interpretation, known as the Red Book. The Orange Book had three aims: to give users a yardstick for how far they can trust a computer system with classified or sensitive information, to tell manufacturers what to build into trusted products, and to provide a basis for writing security requirements when buying systems.

The criteria cover two kinds of requirement, specific security features and assurance, and they define four divisions with seven classes. From highest to lowest:
- Division A, Verified Protection: class A1.
- Division B, Mandatory Protection: classes B3 (Security Domains), B2 (Structured Protection) and B1 (Labeled Security Protection).
- Division C, Discretionary Protection: classes C2 (Controlled Access Protection) and C1 (Discretionary Security Protection).
- Division D, Minimal Protection: class D.
Products in higher classes have more security mechanisms and are checked more rigorously.

The TCSEC had limits. It was written for operating systems and did not suit other products well. It reflected the needs of the US government and military, which paid for it. Every class except C1 and C2 requires mandatory access control, which most commercial organisations do not use. It also ignored integrity, availability and other needs of business applications. Even so, it created a process for evaluating commercial products and raised awareness of computer security, and products such as virus checkers, firewalls and virtual private networks followed in the 1990s.

Europe developed its own criteria, ITSEC (version 1.2 was published in 1991), because the Orange Book was seen as too rigid; ITSEC gave more weight to integrity and availability. Both were later replaced by the Common Criteria, an international ISO standard that combines the TCSEC, ITSEC and the Canadian and US federal criteria. The Common Criteria formally took the place of the Orange Book in 2005.
