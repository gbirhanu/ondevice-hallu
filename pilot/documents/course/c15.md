# The Bell-LaPadula and Biba models

- Document: c15 (course)
- Source: Written for this project from the topics of the CoSc4035 Computer Security course slides (Jimma University), Chapters 6 and 7
- Licence: CC BY 4.0

Multilevel security is based on the idea that some data is more sensitive than other data. Subjects (users and processes) and objects (files and other data) are given security levels, such as Top Secret, Secret, Confidential and Unclassified, and a subject's level decides which objects it may access. The two best-known multilevel models are the Bell-LaPadula model and the Biba model.

The Bell-LaPadula model, developed by David Elliott Bell and Leonard LaPadula, was one of the first multilevel security models. It was created to control access to classified data in government and military systems, and it protects confidentiality. It has two basic rules:
- No read up (the simple security property): a subject may only read objects at its own level or below.
- No write down (the star property): a subject may only write to objects at its own level or above. This stops secret information from flowing into less protected files.
For example, a user cleared only for Confidential cannot read Top Secret personnel files, and a user with Top Secret clearance cannot write into Confidential activity logs, even though she may read them.

The Bell-LaPadula model does not protect integrity; in fact, its star property lets a low-level subject write into a highly classified object. Kenneth Biba proposed integrity policies to deal with this, and his strict integrity policy is what is usually called the Biba model. It reverses the Bell-LaPadula rules:
- No read down (the simple integrity property): a subject can only read objects whose integrity level is the same as or higher than its own, so a trusted subject is not contaminated by less trustworthy data.
- No write up (the integrity star property): a subject can only write to objects whose integrity level is the same as or lower than its own, so it cannot corrupt more reliable data. This limits the damage a Trojan horse can do.
Military policies usually focus on confidentiality and commercial policies on integrity. The two models can in theory be combined, but then subjects can only read and write at their own level, so it is usually better to choose one.
